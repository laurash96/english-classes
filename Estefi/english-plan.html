Why this plan exists

Estefi is not a true beginner. Her diagnostic scored 23/30 (76.7%) — Vocabulary 100%, Reading 100%, Situational English 80%. She recognized First Conditional, Present Perfect + "since", and the modal "should" without hesitation. A Pre-A1 "Hello, World" plan would have bored her in the first ten minutes and, worse, would have signaled that her English is worth less than it is.

Her gaps are structural, not conceptual. All seven misses map to specific grammar points, not a lack of vocabulary or comprehension. That means the right approach is targeted consolidation through real tasks — not re-teaching English from the ground up.

Every class opens with her own diagnostic answer. Instead of "today we study Present Perfect," the hook always starts from the exact sentence she got wrong (or left blank). This does two things: it respects that she already tried and got close, and it makes the correction land as "oh, THAT's what that means" instead of a lecture.

Tone reminder: Never frame this as remedial. She is closing specific gaps on the way to a fluency she mostly already has. Avoid words like "beginner," "basic," or "level" in front of her — the whole point of naming this course after her instead of a CEFR code is that she should feel like this is bespoke, not corrective.
📊
Diagnostic snapshot

Full results: Vocabulary 6/6 (100%) · Basic Grammar 6/8 (75%) · Intermediate Grammar 3/6 (50%) · Reading 5/5 (100%) · Situational English 4/5 (80%).

The weak spot is concentrated almost entirely in Intermediate Grammar — which is exactly where this plan spends most of its time (Classes 1, 4, 5, 6). Classes 2 and 3 patch two Basic Grammar misses that are quick to fix but easy to leave unaddressed forever if nobody targets them directly.

Skills the diagnostic could not test — listening, speaking fluency, pronunciation, spontaneous production — should be observed informally throughout these six classes and may reshape the plan for whatever comes after Class 6.

🧵
Cohesion thread — connectors

Observed in live classes, not in the written diagnostic: Estefi tends to speak in short, disconnected chunks — accurate words strung together without and, but, because, so holding them into a sentence. This is a fluency/cohesion gap, not a grammar one, so it doesn't get its own class. Instead, a small "connector toolkit" is layered into the four classes that already involve extended storytelling (2, 4, 5, 6), growing in sophistication each time.

How to push it live: Don't pre-teach the list like a vocabulary sheet. Let her tell the story choppy first, then ask "can you connect those two sentences?" and offer the one connector that fits. Praise the connected version specifically — the goal is for her to notice for herself that it sounds more fluent, not to memorize a rule.

Session 01 · 60 minutes
Right Now vs. Every Day
Present Simple vs. Present Continuous
Simple vs. Continuous
From her diagnostic — Q10
"Look! The children ___ in the garden."
She chose: plays (B)Correct: are playing (C)
🔥
The Hook — Warm-up8 min

Activity: "Caught on Camera"

1
Ask her to describe what's happening in the room around her right now: "My dog is sleeping." "The neighbor is drilling something." Push for 3–4 sentences before explaining anything.
2
Show her the Q10 sentence from her diagnostic: "Look! The children ___ in the garden." Ask: "Why 'are playing' and not 'play'? What's the difference?" Let her theorize before confirming — she has the intuition, she just needs the label.
3
Land the rule in one line: "Right now → be + -ing. Always / usually / every day → base verb." No further metalanguage needed.
⚡
The Task — Main Challenge30 min

Challenge: "Narrate My Life"

Two rounds that force the two tenses to compete for airtime, so the contrast has to become automatic rather than memorized.

1
Round 1 — live narration (12 min): Open her page (students.html) to the scene deck and hit random together — she narrates what's happening as if commentating live: "A man is crossing the street. Two kids are running." Push for at least 8 sentences, all Present Continuous.
2
Round 2 — the swap (12 min): Same photo, new question: "Does this happen every day in this city, or is it unusual?" She now has to switch into Present Simple to generalize: "People usually walk fast here. Kids don't normally run in the street." Flag every accidental switch back to -ing without correcting mid-sentence — collect them for the error round.
3
Debrief (6 min): Ask her directly: "When do you reach for -ing without thinking, and when do you reach for the base verb?" Naming her own pattern sticks better than being told the rule twice.
Stative verb trap to watch for: Because she's about to get comfortable with -ing, watch for overcorrection into verbs that never take it: "I'm liking this," "I'm knowing him." A quick contrast is enough: "like, know, want, need, love — these verbs don't wear -ing. Ever."

She chose: be (D)Correct: were (B)
🔥
The Hook — Warm-up8 min

Activity: "Then and Now"

1
Open her page to the venue deck and hit random together. Ask: "What's in this picture?" Build 3–4 sentences with "there is / there are."
2
Reveal her Q13 sentence: "There ___ a lot of people at the party." Ask her to fix it herself first, then confirm: past plural needs "were," not "be." "Be" is the infinitive — it never goes alone in a real sentence.
⚡
The Task — Main Challenge30 min

Challenge: "The Best Party You've Been To"

1
Present description (8 min): Describe her current living room or workspace using "there is / there are / there isn't / there aren't." Push past the obvious objects into detail: "There's a plant that's always dying."
2
Past narrative (16 min): She describes the best party, concert, or event she's ever been to, using "there was / there were" throughout: "There were a lot of people. There was really good music. There wasn't enough food." Let her run long here — this is real storytelling, not a drill. The first pass will likely come out as disconnected fragments — that's expected, and it's exactly what step 3 is for.
3
Connect it (6 min): Play back two of her fragment sentences and ask: "Can you connect those with 'and,' 'but,' 'because,' or 'so'?" — e.g. "There were a lot of people, but the music was too loud, so we left early." Don't pre-teach the four words as a list; pull them out one at a time, only when a real sentence needs one.

The Hook — Warm-up8 min

Activity: "Modal or Not?"

1
Rapid-fire: say pairs of sentences aloud ("I can to dance" / "I can dance") and ask her to point at the correct one, no thinking time. This surfaces the intuition before naming the rule.
2
Show her Q14. State the rule once, cleanly: "Modal + base verb. No 'to.' No exceptions — can, should, must, will, would."
⚡
The Task — Main Challenge30 min

Challenge: "Dear Estefi" — the advice column

1
Set-up (4 min): Explain she's now an advice columnist. You'll pull real-sounding problems from the deck in her page and she answers each one on the spot, out loud.
2
Give advice, 4 rounds (18 min): Draw 4 problems from her page's deck (random or picked). For each one she must use at least one modal: "You should talk to your boss." "You must get more sleep." "You don't have to say yes every time." Push for variety across should / must / have to / don't have to across the four rounds.
3
Her turn to ask (8 min): She brings a real (or invented) problem of her own and you answer using modals, modeling natural register: "Honestly? You should just tell her directly."

Session 04 · 60 minutes
I've Never Told You This
Present Perfect (experience) vs. Past Simple (specific story)
Present Perfect
From her diagnostic — Q15
"I ___ this movie three times."
She chose: am seeing (D)Correct: have seen (B)
🔥
The Hook — Warm-up8 min

Activity: "Two Truths and a Lie — Perfect edition"

1
She gives you 3 statements about her life experiences, all starting with "I've": "I've been to three countries." "I've never eaten sushi." "I've met a famous person." You guess the lie.
2
Show her Q15. Point out: she already nailed "since 2020" in Q16 — Present Perfect isn't new to her, it's specifically the "unspecified repeated experience" use ("three times," no date attached) that trips her up. Contrast: "I have seen it three times (we don't know exactly when) vs. I saw it last Friday (exact time, so Past Simple)."
⚡
The Task — Main Challenge30 min

Challenge: "Have You Ever…?" — then tell me about it

1
Round 1 — the interview (12 min): Draw from her page's deck for a rapid series of "Have you ever…?" questions. She answers with "Yes, I have" / "No, I haven't / I never have."
2
Round 2 — zoom in (14 min): For every "yes," immediately follow up with a Past Simple question that forces the switch: "When did that happen? What happened?" She now has to tell the specific story in Past Simple. This pairing — Present Perfect to open, Past Simple to narrate — is the exact pattern native speakers use and it's the one her diagnostic showed she doesn't yet control. Push her to sequence the events rather than list them: "What happened first? And then?"
3
Swap (4 min): She asks you 2 "Have you ever…?" questions, you answer and let her follow up with the "when/what happened" question herself.

✓ I have seen this movie three times.
Her Q15 answer. "Three times" with no date is a Present Perfect signal, not Present Continuous — nothing is happening right now.

The Hook — Warm-up8 min

Activity: "Where Were You When…?"

1
Ask her a classic "where were you when X happened" question about something memorable (a big news event, hearing important personal news). Model the answer shape first: "I was having lunch when I heard about it."
2
Show her Q18. Since she left it blank rather than guessing wrong, treat this gently as "let's build the pattern from scratch, no correction needed" — she avoided the mistake, she just needs the tool. Give the shape once: "long background action (was/were + -ing) + short action that interrupts it (Past Simple)."
⚡
The Task — Main Challenge30 min

Challenge: "Interrupted" — shared doc narrative

Uses the shared class5-template.html writing template.

1
Brainstorm (5 min): Together, list 4–5 real interrupted moments from her life. If she blanks, hit random on the idea jogger in her page for a nudge — swap in her own real version of whatever it jogs loose.
2
Write (15 min): In the shared doc, she writes 4 short "interrupted moment" stories using "I was ___-ing when ___." Real events land better than invented ones — encourage specificity (what exactly interrupted it, how she reacted).
3
Read aloud + oral retelling (10 min): She reads two stories aloud, then retells a third one from memory with the doc closed — this is where the structure moves from "written and checked" to "spoken and automatic." Once "while/when" feel solid, offer the sharper variants from the connector toolkit for the retelling — "that's when" and "as soon as" make the story sound noticeably less choppy.

✓ I was watching TV when my phone rang.
Her Q18 gap. The interrupting action is always Past Simple — it's a single completed moment, not a habit.

Session 06 · 60 minutes
The Place Where It Happened
Relative clauses (where / who / which) · How often vs. How long
Wrap-up
From her diagnostic — Q20 and Q28
Q20: "This is the restaurant ___ we had dinner last week." (unanswered, correct: where)
Q28: "How often do you exercise?"
She chose: For two hours (A)Correct: Three times a week (C)
🔥
The Hook — Warm-up8 min

Activity: "Frequency or Duration?"

1
Fire quick questions at her: "How often do you check your phone?" vs. "How long have you had that phone?" Have her notice out loud that one asks "how many times" and the other asks "for what span of time."
2
Show her Q28 and Q20 together. For Q20, point out this is the same relative pronoun family as "who" (people) and "which" (things) — "where" is just the version for places, and it replaces having to say "in that restaurant" a second time.
⚡
The Task — Main Challenge28 min

Challenge: "The Place Where…" + rapid-fire interview

1
Relative clause storytelling (14 min): She describes 3 meaningful places using "This is the place where…": where she grew up, where she met someone important, where something memorable happened. Push her to also try one "who" (a person who…) and one "which" (a thing which…) for full coverage.
2
Frequency vs. duration interview (14 min): Rapid back-and-forth interview drawing from her page's deck — mixed "How often…?" and "How long…?" questions, each tagged 🔁/⏳. She must answer each with the matching answer type (a count vs. a span). Push at least one answer into a contrast: "I call her every day, although we don't always have much to say."

✓ This is the restaurant where we had dinner.

The full monologue · 60–90 seconds, no notes

One voice note that touches all six classes: something happening right now, a place with "there is/are," a piece of advice with a modal, an experience with Present Perfect, a short interrupted story, and one sentence with "the place where…" or a "how often / how long" answer. This time, require at least 4 different connectors from across the course (and/but/because/so, first/then/after that, as soon as/that's when, although/actually) — the monologue should flow as one connected piece, not a list of correct but separate sentences.
